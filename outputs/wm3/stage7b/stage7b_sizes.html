Does a bigger driver policy earn more?

Stage 7b, part 2 · world model 3 · ride_world · San Francisco and Pune · same market as stage 7b (half the idle drivers off, demand ×3, menus of up to 6 rides every 2 min)

In short
  • No. The 60M-weight policy ties the 0.6M one in San Francisco and is worse in Pune; the 30M one is below both.
  • Round 30, net pay per online hour of the 25% of drivers on the agent, over the same drivers taking the first ride (wins out of 12 held-out starts):
    policySan FranciscoPune
    0.6M+0.887 ± 0.095 (12/12)+0.067 ± 0.008 (12/12)
    30M+0.644 ± 0.055 (12/12)+0.057 ± 0.008 (12/12)
    60M+0.902 ± 0.071 (12/12)+0.036 ± 0.010 (10/12)
  • The large policies only train safely with small steps. The first two 30M runs collapsed into driving around empty (hundreds of self-moves) until the training objective was bounded, the learning rate lowered and each round's change capped.
  • Every policy serves more riders than taking the first ride in San Francisco; the 60M one serves fewer in Pune. Drivers still taking the first ride lose a little with every policy.
  • The 0.6M policy stays the default: as good or better, 50–100× cheaper per decision. The limit is the market and the reward, not the size of the network.

All trained with GRPO inside the world model, every driver on the current policy, 24 SF + 16 Pune branched choices per round; the large ones decide on the GPU in half precision. After the fixes, every run uses a bounded (dual-clip) surrogate and stops a round's epochs once it moved the policy more than KL 0.03.

1. The gain, round by round

What it shows

  • Each point: 12 held-out starts, 60 imagined minutes, 25% of drivers on the policy and the rest taking the first ride, compared with everyone taking the first ride (± s.e.).
  • Colour and marker shape both mark the policy.

How to read it

  • Round 0 is the starting policy, close to taking the first ride, so the gain is about zero.
  • The 60M line is flat until round 10 because its learning rate was too small; after raising it, it catches up in San Francisco.
  • In Pune drivers have fewer rides to choose from (about 1.5 per menu), so every gain is about ten times smaller.

2. How the policies changed while training

What it shows

  • Left and middle: how often the first ride is the policy's most likely choice, from the training rollouts.
  • Right: how far the policy has moved from where it started (KL divergence, log scale).

How to read it

  • Learning means moving away from the first ride: the lines fall as the policies learn to pick better-paying rides.
  • The bigger the policy, the smaller the steps it can take safely, so it moves away later.

3. The 30M collapses and the fix

What it shows

  • Three runs of the same 30M policy with the same random numbers, which is why they agree up to round 10.
  • Left: the size of the training objective. Middle: distance from the starting policy. Right: the held-out gain in San Francisco.

How to read it

  • Run 1: the objective reached 1027. Every action is tried, and for one the policy had almost never picked, PPO's term is unbounded; it took over the update and the policy collapsed by round 30.
  • Run 2: with the objective bounded it still collapsed, at round 20, because each round moved the big policy too far.
  • Run 3: a lower learning rate and a cap on each round's change keep it healthy to round 30.

4. What the policies do to the city

What it shows

  • Round 30. The first two panels put every driver on the policy; the third has 25% on the policy and shows the other 75%.
  • All against the same start with every driver taking the first ride.

How to read it

  • Choosing better rides serves more riders in San Francisco (+14 to +18%) at the cost of slightly longer waits.
  • In Pune the 60M policy turns down so many rides (about 1,900 in the 60 imagined minutes, against about 270 for the 0.6M policy) that fewer riders are picked up.
  • Drivers who keep taking the first ride lose a little: the agent takes the better rides first.
